Doctoral Dissertation by TAKAHARA Taichi　（１）

Ameblo · 2022年9月15日 · Original page / 元ページ

This series of English articles aims to offer a brief version of Japanese articles about “Re-examination of the Movement against US Airbase Expansion in Tachikawa: From “Bloody Sunagawa” to Multi-faceted Historical Image.” The first two of them were posted on July 26 and August 10, 2022.


This doctoral dissertation written by TAKAHARA Taichi was highly regarded as “an original contribution to the history of social movements” by the review committee of Tokyo University of Foreign Studies in February 2022.
Here is a preliminary attempt to outline its preface and first chapter below:
 

 From “Bloody Sunagawa” to Multi-faceted Historical Image

Objective and Research Materials


Takahara presents an alternative to conventional narratives like the ones in textbooks of history. He draws more attention to the locals’ community-based, daily life-oriented practices and emotions rather than the direct activism usually associated with the image of bloody Sunagawa.


For this purpose, he analyzes a wider collection of documents, including women’s essays and school children’s compositions, as well as official records of statements made by the leaders of the Sunagawa Anti Base Expansion Alliance at court or in the Diet committee sessions. 
In other words, Takahara’s dissertation is a challenge to the almost legendary story of “Sunagawa Toso (Struggle),” and that seems to be the reason why he carefully avoids the term or puts it in brackets, showing a preference of more lengthy and descriptive naming as was adopted in the title of his disseration.
Reference Materials and Preceding Researches


Takahara reviews major preceding academic researches relevant to the Sunagawa Struggle to divide them by relative inclination or trends into two categories: one tends to focus on supporters’ activism with an emphasis on the victory as a result of the united actions by landowners and supporters. This trend is represented by SHIMIZU Ikutaro, an eminent scholar of the time, KOYAMA Hirotake and TAKABATAKE Michitoshi, historians who stressed the importance of Zengakuren, the All-Japan Federation of Students' Self-Government Associations. Generally speaking, they praised the farmers for their “growth” or “development” to become “progressive,” or probably more politically conscious.
 

The other category of research is more or less critical of the first group. They were interested in the locality of Sunagawa from the viewpoint of regional history and the internal factors within the Alliance. As a monumental work on Sunagawa in this second group, 

Takahara refers to a paper written by a historian AOKI Kenichi and published in 1969. More than ten years after the official decision to discontinue the land survey in 1956, the Alliance became smaller in size and yet they had kept the Japanese government and the US authorities from expanding the base as they had intended to. Then, Aoki tried to identify the fundamental factors which brought Sunagawa such a successful victory, rather than attributing the victory of 1956 to a single factor such as the supports from outside as Koyama did. Obviously, the meaning of “victory” was different between them.
Takahara recognizes Aoki’s original efforts to interview not only the members of the Alliance but also others surrounding them so that the composition or the silhouette of the Alliance stand out.


Another notable researcher in the second group is a political scientist AKETAGAWA Toru, who wrote a dissertation in 2000 based on his analysis of resolutions and statements announced in the assemblies organized by the Alliance. Thus, in Takahara’s perception, Aketagawa pointed out that the alliance advocated the land preservation based on the Constitution and on such values as “the land descended from ancestors” or the farmers “livelihood.” Judging from the minutes of national rally of anti-base movements, Aketagawa also noted that representatives of Sunagawa positively set the logic of peace and independence or the reasoning for cancelling the Japan- US Security Treaty against the Cold War logic or the logic of the Security Treaty. This suggests that their logical grounds for opposing the base expansion apparently changed in time.


Another historian MATSUDA Keisuke, in his dissertation published in 2007, pointed out that the logic of opposition had developed and diversified. He concluded that there still existed something consistent and common behind their logic, that is “local patriotism,” or affection for their homeland. 
It was YOSHIMI Toshiya, an urban sociologist, who analyzed the reality of the homeland. He attached importance to agricultural character or local context of Tama area, which Sunagawa belongs to in a geographically wider perspective. Takahara observes that Yoshimi offered an alternative viewpoint about the region beyond the limited locality of Sunagawa. 
Significance of Takahara’s Own Research


Takahara’s own work is positioned in a field of subjects crossing over the boundaries between the two trends in the earlier research. It was between 2010 and 2021 that he continued his research and he witnessed the 55th anniversary of the Sunagawa Struggle. This turning point triggered alternative narratives or rephrased speeches of the people who experienced the struggle, which developed into movements to inherit memories and histories. Few as the number was, a younger generation of concerned citizens (or maybe community members) are now able to review the anti-base movements of their parents and grandparents in terms of their life-long mental or spiritual history. Over sixty years has passed since the events took place, and in the process of shifting from the memory to the history, Takahara is aware that inner aspects or thoughts of the individuals engaged in the protests are yet to be examined. Obviously, this finding has motivated him to accomplish this research.
Logic of “Absolute Opposition” and Disobedience


In the first chapter of his doctoral dissertation, Takahara attempts to reveal why the farmers in Sunagawa established a stance to be absolutely against the expansion plan at the very beginning and how it was maintained by the protesters till the end. He also examines how, or whether, their theoretical grounding changed, or not.
His argument here is mainly based on his analysis of the statements made by AOKI Ichigoro, a leader of the action corps of the Alliance who was invited to three cabinet committee sessions of the Diet during the three-month period from June to September 1955. Takahara reveals first that Aoki expressed opposition due to the problems that expansion would cause to the farmers’ lifeways, such as possible discontinuation or relocation of farming. 

Then Takahara points out that Aoki maintained “absolute opposition,” referring to the historical facts, including the air raids on August 2, 1945, during the World War II, unjustifiable land seizure during the post-war occupation period, and other land requisitions that the farmers were forced to obey. Those are perceived as a reflection of farmers’ inherent considerations in the process of forming the logic of “absolute opposition” or no more obedience whatsoever.
