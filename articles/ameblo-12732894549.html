(2)　About Sunagawa Toso (struggle) 

砂川闘争

 

Every time the Procurement Bureau of Tokyo sent the local survey team to Sunagawa, the farmers tried to keep them out by way of nonviolent disobedience, engaging in sit-downs, staging demonstrations and even using bamboos coated with night soils.

But the survey team and police persistently came back, causing the local residents to spend uneasy days.

In September 13 and 14, 1955, a survey team guarded by 1800 policemen pushed through the land measurement, resulting in violent crashes with the landowners and supporters who physically resisted it by forming a tight scrum. In this single incident, thirty protestors were arrested and more than one hundred got injured.

Consequently, the Procurement Bureau succeeded in driving survey stakes to mark the outline of extended US base for the official certification of the land expropriation.

It was a shocking defeat on part of the protestors, but AOKI Ichigoro, captain of Sunagawa Action Corps, demonstrated their unyielding will, saying “They drove stakes in our land, but they shall never drive one in our mind.” 

Increasing number of journalists and scholars had been watching with serious concern the police’s violence used to the unarmed protestors. On December 17, 1955, and again on January 27, 1956, groups of prominent intellectuals visited Sunagawa and soon initiated a gathering to discuss issues of military bases. 

Zengakuren, the All-Japan Federation of Students' Self-Government Associations, announced their support for the anti-base alliance of Sunagawa in September, 1956. Thus, thousands of supporters were regularly mobilized, with some of them stationed on the farm to assist farmers or to attend their children. 

The fiercest battle was observed on October 12 and 13, 1956, as was depicted in KAMEI Fumio’s film Record of Blood: Sunagawa. The ongoing land survey was scheduled to continue until October 16.

On October 14, in order to secure the defense against the escalating violence of the police’s attack, the alliance decided to mobilize ten thousand workers and students. 

And on that night, the Japanese Government reached a decision to discontinue the survey on the farmland.

With this apparent victory, the supporting union members under the influence of the Socialist Party, which was the largest opposition party in those days, left Sunagawa to fight for other causes in other parts of Japan. 

But this victory turned out to be only the beginning of even more lengthy and relentless struggles of Sunagawa.

As a matter of fact, Sunagawa Toso was a multifaceted struggle from its initial stage and court battles also played a crucial part of it. 

 It was MIYAOKA Masao, sub-captain of Sunagawa Action Corps, that committed himself to and devoted more time and efforts for litigation strategies than anybody else. In cooperation with democratic and progressive young lawyers, he exercised all possible legal rights and procedures.
