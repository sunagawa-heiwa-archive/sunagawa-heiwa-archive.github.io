Continuing from the article posted on September 15, 2022, here is the second part of an English summary of TAKAHARA Taichi’s doctoral dissertation, “Re-examination of the Movement against US Airbase Expansion in Tachikawa: From “Bloody Sunagawa” to Multi-faceted Historical Image.”

Doctoral Dissertation by TAKAHARA Taichi　（１）

The article below is a summary of the original dissertation’s Chapter 2 and 3 combined.

 Sunagawa Issues Viewed and Experienced by the Intellectuals of the Time

Historian TAKAHASHI Shinichi’s Engagement


The Sunagawa Struggle was known not only for its physical activism but also for its ideological and psychological influences. The intellectuals and journalists who visited the site and witnessed the violence used by the police, reported their observation in the mass media, thereby increasing the political awareness of the general public. They formed a loosely united group to meet and discuss issues of military bases. It was named “Kichi Mondai Bunka-jin Kondan-kai (cultural figures’ meeting to talk about base issues).”


One of its members TAKAHASHI Shinichi was a historian and history teacher whom the author TAKAHARA Taichi features in Chapter 2 of his dissertation. Takahashi contributed his report on Sunagawa to the Sekai (the world), a monthly magazine well known among intelligent citizens and students, as well as to academic periodicals specializing in history education.
“Reportage: Sunagawa on September 13”


On this day in 1955 Takahashi first visited Sunagawa and witnessed when a survey stake was driven for the first time after four-months of resistance by the Sunagawa locals’ alliance against base expansion. Takahashi collected information through his on-site observation, and contributed a reportage to the November issue of the Sekai. From that point forward, he became deeply involved in Sunagawa for more than 20 years.
In his article, Takahashi described the first scenes he saw early in the morning of the day; a farmer smiling with a red flag on his shoulder. Then, someone suddenly called out “they’re coming!” and the face-to-face confrontation started between union workers and policemen. And yet according to his description, local farmers and supporters remained in good spirits. Some were rather friendly with policemen, one lighting a cigarette for the other, and some college students casually chatted with policemen, both smiling. 


Takahashi’s report also focuses on the police, referring to an individual’s face and attitude, rather than depicting them as a group of uniformity. For instance, Takahashi’s attention was attracted by a policeman, who was standing still with tears in his eyes, perhaps wrestling with something in his mind. And at the end of the day, on his way home from Sunagawa, Takahashi writes, he was asking himself “how are you going to respond to the issues of Sunagawa as a historian and educator?”
Takahara, the author of the dissertation, is particularly concerned with this kind of perturbation Takahashi experienced in Sunagawa and expressed in his writings.
Speech on September 25, 1955, at Academic Meeting

In his speech on September 25, 1955 at the annual convention of history education, Takahashi presented the details of what happened in Sunagawa as part of a contemporary history he learned with his own eyes and ears. Takahashi took the tears of a young policeman as an example of complexity and contradiction of the contemporary history to illustrate what was overlooked by the post-war history※. 

He raised a question, “in our effort to promote a scientific history by dispelling the mythological narratives of the pre-war history, did we, parents, teachers and even historians, sufficiently acquire history-conscious awareness to grasp the scientific history through empathetic understanding of the people’s real life?” Thus, Takahara points out, Takahashi proposed a transformation of historical understanding, or the conceptual conversion of history.
※According to the speech which was reproduced in his book 歴史教育論 Rekishi Kyoiku Ron, published in 1955, Takahashi quoted local farmer’s words and phrases as follows: “Just now you twisted up our arms, didn’t you? Kicked our legs, didn’t you? Ain’t you from farmer families, too? Your Dads and Moms back at home are threshing rice at this moment, ain’t they? Can you twist up the arm of your Dad and Mom just as you did to us now? Can you kick their leg?......” Then, Takahashi saw one policeman shamelessly sticking out his tongue, another restlessly moving around, and another standing still as if wrestling with something in his mind with tears in his eyes.
Discussion with Local Teachers on October 31, 1955


Takahashi was also interested in local teachers’ Sunagawa issues. On October 31, 1955, when he participated as a moderator in a round-table discussion of school teachers, he referred to a policeman’s facial expression again. He said that during a lunch break on September 13, he found a policeman eating his lunch as if chewing sand, looking like the most wretched person on earth. “If the policeman is regarded as an enemy, then is it possible to solve the problems of Sunagawa?” he questioned. But a teacher of Sunagawa Junior High School replied, saying “We also think the power of all the people is important, so we try to do in accordance with what all the people can do, but that’s not possible.”


Takahara observes that there existed an apparent perception gap between a theorist Takahashi and practitioners like school teachers. On the other hand, Takahara assumes that the question of what teachers could do when they faced a power or problems surpassing their capabilities, must have reminded Takahashi of his experience as a teacher during the years of wartime militarism and during the red purge of the post-war era.
School Teachers’ Efforts for Inclusion
Based on the records kept by the Sunagawa alliance against base-expansion and supporting labor unions as well as local teachers’ essays, TAKAHARA Taichi pays attention to the various activities conducted by teachers of Sunagawa Junior High, which was the only secondary school in the town. They belonged to Nikkyoso, the Japan Teachers’ Union, which was regarded as a leftist organization by conservatives. Just about one year before the outbreak of the Sunagawa Struggle, Japan’s Ministry of Education amended two laws to restrict education civil servants’ freedom of political activities. 
Nevertheless, some committed school teachers called upon other teachers at two elementary schools in Sunagawa to participate in a general rally to demonstrate the town’s protest. 


The rally’s slogan was carefully designed to circumvent the Ministry of Education restrictions by emphasizing the importance of environmental protection and the preservation of children’s rights and quality of life. A teacher specific group was also organized and recognized by the local education committee. 
However, when the rally’s statement opposing the base expansion was to be read aloud, some teachers who were not union members, secretly consulted the police about it, probably because of the influence of the new educational laws enacted in the previous year. The police immediately responded, denouncing the group of teachers, characterizing them as a secret society. Under those pressures, the teacher’s group was disbanded within a day.
As the Procurement Agency of Tokyo successfully persuaded more landowners to accept increasingly favorable conditions to sell their land, the conflict between those conditional compromisers and uncompromising protestors became more obvious. This adversely affected the relationship between students at Sunagawa Juior High where children of conditional parents, indomitable protestors, and policemen attended. Concerned teachers felt compelled to do something about it and came up with various measures to better understand the Sunagawa issues. 


According to Takahara, they conducted quantitative and qualitative research about the damages caused by roaring sounds of airplanes and also encouraged their students to write free essays to express themselves. As a result, they realized that some issues pertaining to the base expansion should be treated as school-specific problems. This finding led them to initiate a research circle for base and education study in a teachers’ sub-union of Sunagawa Junior High. 
One of the major activities of the circle members was to present the theme of the bases and education to a larger annual meeting of the nationwide Teachers’ Union to start a dialogue and learn from each other.
After they compiled a report for a local meeting, they preceded to the next stage and gave a presentation at a regional meeting of the Teachers’ Union held in Tokyo. 
On this stage, the circle members from Sunawaga entered into a subcommittee that considered how to promote human rights and deepen international understanding. They expected the audience to give feedback on how to deal with the complicated Sunagawa issues from an educational point of view. In fact, nobody was able to give a satisfactory answer, but the circle members were encouraged by a remark of one participant, who said that there was nothing special about Sunagawa as what is happening in Sunagawa is symbolic of the situation that all the Japanese people found themselves in.
Two month later, however, when they got to the final stage of the national meeting of the Teachers’ Union and participated in the subcommittee for education in remote and special areas, the circle members from Sunagawa were more frustrated than ever. In the perception of the organizer, including the subcommittee’s moderator and guest speaker, the base-related issues should be considered not as something specific to a particular region, but in a larger framework or in its universal application to underlying problems in the larger social system of Japan. Reflecting the influence of the grand theory of the time, that is, Marxism, some intellectuals tended to regard universality and determinate laws more important than individual practices and actions, though the Teachers’ Union was not a monolithic organization.
Before closing Chapter 4 of his dissertation, Takahara discusses the later life experiences of the circle members from Sunagawa Junior High. One of them, TAZAWA Yoshiko, attended a 1959 national meeting of the Teachers’ Union, and wrote a report to increase teachers’ awareness of base issues. Takahara notices that even though the circle became smaller and less active, some members were even more positively engaged. Tazawa’s report was also concerned with a gender issue; thanks to the circle members’ efforts, it was not so burdensome for her to be a working mother, but she found it emotionally frustrating to stay active while leaving her child at home.
More details of such factors as child-school and woman-home will be developed in the following 2 chapters, that is, in (3) of this English abstract series of articles.
